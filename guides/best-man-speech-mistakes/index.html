WRITING THE SPEECH

Best man speech mistakes and how to avoid them

Updated 18 November 2026

About these guides: written by Best Man Backup, an independent site for best men only.

THE SHORT ANSWER

The mistakes that sink speeches are usually structural, not a missing joke: too long, too many thin stories, no line about the partner, burying your face in a phone, and drinking courage until the mic arrives. Fix the shape first, then polish lines.

Mistake 1: Treating “what not to say” as the only risk

What not to say covers content landmines. This page covers craft and delivery failures that still hurt even when the material is clean.

Mistake 2: Too long

Most speeches land at three to five minutes; five to seven is fine for a main UK speaker; ten is the ceiling. See how long a best man speech should be. Fix: time a standing read. Cut the second version of every joke.

Mistake 3: Six stories, zero point

A list of anecdotes feels like a list. Pick a trait and prove it once or twice — how to pick stories.

Mistake 4: Forgetting the partner

If they only appear in the toast, the speech feels like a friendship roast. Name them early and give one specific observation.

Mistake 5: Phone script, face down

Screens lock, glow and hide your eyes. Use cue cards or learn beats without notes.

Mistake 6: No ending

“Anyway, that’s me” is not a close. Signal the finish, turn to the couple, raise the glass — how to end and toast examples.

Mistake 7: Panic drinking and zero rehearsal

One drink if it helps; water until you have spoken. Rehearse standing up. Nerves plan: speech nerves.

The Speech Kit exists to stop these mistakes early: story prompts, a workbook, and cards so you are not inventing structure the night before.

Mistake 8: inside jokes as a personality

If you need a footnote for “Big Dave”, the room has already left. Inside jokes feel like warmth to the three people who get them and like exclusion to everyone else. Translate the joke into a scene anyone can follow, or cut it.

Mistake 9: borrowing a speech from the internet

Templates are for structure. Finished speeches you found online are for someone else’s friends. Guests can hear a generic cadence. Steal the shape from our template, not someone else’s childhood.

Mistake 10: ignoring the room you are actually in

A pub garden of forty people and a ballroom of two hundred need different volume, length and formality. Ask about the microphone. Ask who else is speaking. Ask whether speeches are before food, during, or after. Wrong assumptions create the “why is he still talking while starters go cold” failure mode.

A pre-flight checklist

  • Timed standing read within the couple’s limit
  • Partner named with one specific line
  • One main story, safety-tested
  • Toast wording exact, both names correct
  • Cards or anchors ready; phone on silent as backup only
  • Drink limit agreed with yourself before the reception

Mistake 11: apologising your way through the speech

“I’m not funny”, “I hate public speaking”, “this will be awful” — said once, lightly, can earn goodwill. Said three times, it becomes the theme. The room wants you to begin. Begin.

Mistake 12: winging the toast

People remember the last twenty seconds. If you invent the toast, you may forget a name. Write it. Practise it. Put both names on the card in letters you can read in dim light.

Recovery when you have already made a mistake

If a joke dies, pause and move on. If you skip a section, do not announce the skip. If you offend and know it immediately, a short sincere correction beats a long defence. Then finish the toast cleanly. Sitting down well repairs more than you think.

A one-page anti-mistake summary

Write short. Prove one trait. Name the partner. End on the toast. Rehearse standing up. Limit the drink. Use cards or anchors. Safety-test every story. That is most of the job. Everything else is polish.

Alcohol and timing mistakes

Drinking through the reception before you speak is the classic self-own. Cap yourself early. Eat something. Hold a glass of water at the mic if you need a prop. Timing mistakes are quieter: starting while staff are still clearing, or running so long that the next speaker is squeezed. Watch the room and the schedule.

If the toastmaster or planner gives you a signal, take it. Ending thirty seconds early is never a failure. Ending three minutes late after the energy has dropped is.

Content mistakes people repeat

Listing every childhood anecdote. Making the partner a footnote. Quoting song lyrics at length. Reading a speech written for someone else’s voice. Each of those is fixable in one edit pass: pick one story, name the partner early, cut the lyrics, and rewrite lines until they sound like something you would say at a pub table.

Tech and prop mistakes

Slides that need venue Wi-Fi, videos that need a dark room, and props that need a table you do not have are common traps. If a prop is not rehearsed in the actual room, leave it out. A clean spoken toast beats a clever idea that fails in front of grandparents.

Phones as teleprompters lock up, dim, and invite notifications. Printed cards or a single folded sheet remain more reliable. Test whatever you bring under the same lighting you will speak in.

Frequently asked questions

What is the biggest best man speech mistake?

Going long without a clear point, or leaving the partner out until the last line.

Is reading from a phone a mistake?

It often is. Cards or practised beats keep your face up and avoid lock-screen disasters.

How many jokes are too many?

If you cannot remember the thread, you have too many. One or two stories plus a few smiles is enough.

Should I apologise for being nervous?

A brief honest line is fine. A long apology becomes the speech.

Can I fix a bad draft quickly?

Yes — cut to one story, add the partner, write the toast, rehearse twice. See the last-minute guide.
