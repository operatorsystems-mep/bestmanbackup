Why cards beat a full script

A full sheet hides your face and invites reading. A phone screen locks mid-sentence. Cards force glances and eye contact. Wedding speechwriters and toast coaches who rank for this topic converge on the same habit: prompts for the middle, exact lines for the open and close.

How to build the set

  1. Finish the draft and time it aloud.
  2. Split into four to six beats: open, story, partner, toast (add thanks only if required).
  3. One card per beat. Three to six short lines max.
  4. Write punchlines and both names in the toast word for word.
  5. Number the cards. Mark [PAUSE] after laughs.
  6. Print a spare set.

Example card:

Card 2 — Story
Parking-lot night / flask / terrible tea
Point: he shows up
[PAUSE]
Turn to Maya

Rehearse with the cards you will hold

  • Stand up. Hold something at mouth height for the mic.
  • Drill open and toast more than the middle.
  • Practise recovering: stop, breathe, find the next label, continue.
  • Do not rewrite the night before unless a fact is wrong.

Frequently asked questions

How many cue cards should a best man use?

Usually four to six: open, story, partner, toast, plus thanks only if needed.

Should cue cards have the full speech?

No. Prompts for the middle; exact wording for punchlines and the toast.

Is it OK to use a phone instead?

Possible, but cards are safer. Phones lock, dim and light your face.

What size text works?

Large enough to read at a glance in dim light — think headings, not footnotes.
