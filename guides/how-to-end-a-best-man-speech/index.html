ENDING YOUR SPEECH

How to end a best man speech

THE SHORT ANSWER

Signal that you're finishing, turn from the groom to the couple, say one sincere line about their future, then ask everyone to raise a glass and say both names clearly. The ending is the part people remember, so write it word for word and practise it more than anything else in the speech.

Why the ending matters most

Guests forget the middle of most speeches. They remember how it started and how it finished. A strong ending also rescues a wobbly speech: if a joke didn't land earlier, a warm, confident close puts you back on solid ground. And it's the moment that's most likely to end up as the clip people share from the wedding video.

The ending also has a practical job. It hands the room to the next part of the day: the cake, the first dance, the bar. A clear finish makes that transition easy for everyone.

The four beats of a good ending

  1. Signal. Let the room know you're nearly done. "Before I finish" or "So, to wrap up" gives people a moment to find their glasses.
  2. Turn to the couple. Most of the speech is about the groom. The end should be about both of them.
  3. One sincere line. No joke here. Say what you actually hope for them.
  4. The toast. Invite everyone to raise a glass, pause, and say both names. Debrett's notes the best man may then announce the cutting of the cake, if that's the plan. Our best man toast guide covers the etiquette, including whether people stand.

How to move from funny to sincere

The hardest part of most speeches is the switch from teasing to meaning it. If it's too sudden, it feels jarring. Three ways to make the turn:

  • Use a bridge phrase. "But in all seriousness..." is fine, though overused. Try "But here's the thing about Tom..." or "Behind all of that, though..."
  • Turn a running joke. If your theme was his lateness, end on it: "He's been late for everything for twenty years. Except meeting you."
  • Tell the room what you noticed. "The first time I saw him with Jess, I noticed he'd stopped checking his phone." A small observed detail is more moving than a big statement.

Closing lines to adapt

Warm

  • "I've watched him become a better man since he met you. I can't wait to see what you two build together."
  • "Some people make each other better just by being in the same room. These two are like that."
  • "The best thing I can wish you is more of exactly what you've got now."
  • "Tom, you've been a brilliant friend to me. Jess, you're about to find out how lucky you are, though I think you already know."

Light

  • "I'll finish the way he finishes every phone call, by suddenly saying goodbye. Please raise your glasses."
  • "I was going to finish with some marriage advice, then I remembered I'm not qualified. So here's a toast instead."
  • "He's spent years asking me for advice and ignoring it. Jess, I have a feeling he'll listen to you."

Short and simple

  • "So please join me in raising your glasses to the happy couple."
  • "Ladies and gentlemen, to the newlyweds."

Using a quote

A quote can work if it's short and genuinely fits the couple, for example a line from a song that matters to them or something one of their parents always says. Avoid the famous quotes everyone has heard at every wedding. A line of your own about them nearly always lands better.

Saying the toast

  • Say "please raise your glasses" and wait until most people have.
  • Say the names in the order the couple prefer. If in doubt, ask them, or use the order on the invitation.
  • Look at the couple, not your notes, for the last line.
  • Any drink works for a toast, water included.

Write the ending first

It sounds backwards, but many best men find it helps to write the last 30 seconds before anything else. Once you know where you're landing, it's easier to choose the stories that lead there. If your ending says "he's the most loyal friend I have", your stories should show his loyalty, even the funny ones.

What not to do at the end

  • Don't end on a joke at the groom's expense. It's the last thing his new in-laws hear.
  • Don't add "anyway, that's it". Finish on the names.
  • Don't skip the toast. Toastmasters lists forgetting to invite people to join the toast as a common mistake.
  • Don't forget to hand back. If there's a host or another speaker, a quick "and now over to..." keeps things moving.
  • Don't rush. Nerves speed people up most at the end. Slow down for the final two lines.
  • Don't introduce something new. The end isn't the place for a new story. Bring the speech home.
  • Don't apologise. "Sorry that went on a bit" undoes all the good work. Just raise the glass.

If you lose your place near the end

It happens. Keep the final lines on their own cue card, in large type, so you can find them instantly. If you blank, go straight to that card: one sincere line and the toast is a complete ending on its own. Nobody knows what you skipped. Our nerves guide has more on recovering mid-speech.

Rehearse the last 30 seconds the most

Practise the ending until you can say it while looking at the couple. Record it on your phone and listen for speed. Then practise it standing up, with a glass in your hand, because holding a glass and cue cards at once is surprisingly awkward the first time.

Frequently asked questions

What do you say at the end of a best man speech?

Signal you're finishing, say one sincere line about the couple's future, then ask guests to raise their glasses and say both names, for example: "Please raise your glasses to Tom and Jess."

Should the best man speech end with a toast?

Yes, at most weddings the best man finishes with a toast to the couple. At UK weddings it's often the last speech, so the toast closes the speeches.

Is it OK to end a best man speech with a quote?

Yes, if it's short and genuinely fits the couple. A line of your own about them usually lands better than a famous quote.

Should I end my best man speech with a joke?

It's better to end sincerely. A light line just before the toast is fine, but the last words should be warm and about the couple, not a joke at the groom's expense.
