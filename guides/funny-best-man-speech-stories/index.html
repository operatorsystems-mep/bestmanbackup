WRITING THE SPEECH

Funny best man speech stories: how to pick ones that land

Updated 18 November 2026

About these guides: written by Best Man Backup, an independent site for best men only.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Choose one or two stories that show character, not a highlight reel of chaos. Run each story through a safety test: would his gran and her parents still like him after hearing it? Tell it with a clear setup, turn and point, then move on. Jokes without a story often feel thinner in a mixed room.

Story first, punchline second

Our funny best man speech guide covers whole-speech laugh structure. This page is narrower: how to choose the anecdotes themselves. Ranking joke lists help with lines; rooms remember scenes.

The safety test

  • Would the couple still enjoy this sober tomorrow?
  • Can half the room follow it without a five-minute prequel?
  • Does it show a trait you admire, even while people laugh?
  • Does it need an ex, a fight, a crime, or the stag do to work? If yes, cut it.

Full off-limits list: what not to say. Clean one-liners: best man speech jokes.

Shapes that tend to work

  • Competence in the wrong place: he packs perfectly for your crisis and forgets his own charger.
  • Loyalty under mild absurdity: he drives across the country for a boring errand.
  • A habit everyone recognises: lateness, snacks, spreadsheets — told once, specifically.
  • The partner as punchline-free upgrade: what got easier or kinder after they arrived.

How to tell it in under a minute

  1. One line of context.
  2. What he did.
  3. The small specific detail (the flask, the label, the text at 1 a.m.).
  4. What it shows — one sentence.
  5. Optional bridge to the partner.

If you need two stories, make them prove the same trait. Different traits in a row feel like a CV.

Workshop example

Weak: “He’s mental on nights out.” Stronger: “When my car died on the M6 at midnight, he drove two hours, brought snacks, and texted the next morning to check the recovery truck had come. He still only admits he was ‘nearby’.”

Same man, different speech. Practise aloud; if you rush the detail, the laugh dies. Delivery help: without notes and cue cards.

Mining for stories without forcing them

Give yourself a week of noticing before you decide. Prompts that shake loose usable scenes:

  • When did he show up when it was inconvenient?
  • What is he famous for among friends that a stranger could still understand?
  • When did you first meet his partner, and what did you notice without being told?
  • What habit of his became funny only after it helped someone?

Ask one sibling or long friend for a story you do not have. Credit them lightly (“his sister told me…”) so it does not sound stolen.

Editing a story that is almost right

If a story is funny but cruel, remove the cruelty and keep the detail. If it is kind but vague, add one concrete object or time of day. If it needs three characters the room has never met, cut the extras or cut the story.

Read it aloud to someone who does not know the friendship. Wherever they look confused, you have work. Wherever they look uncomfortable, you have a cut.

Placing the story in the speech

Open, then story, then partner, then toast. Do not open with the story before anyone knows who you are. Do not stack a second unrelated story after a strong first one unless it sharpens the same point. End the story on the trait, not on “anyway”. Bridge to the partner with one sentence that uses that trait.

Stress-testing with the right audience

Do not road-test a risky story in the stag WhatsApp. Test it with one person who will be honest and who likes the couple. Ask three questions only: where did you laugh, where did you wince, where were you bored? Act on all three.

If the only person who loves the story is you, that is data. Speeches are for the room, not for your favourite memory.

From notes to a tellable minute

Write the story once in full. Then cut every sentence that is not setup, action, detail or point. Then tell it without the paper. Then put three prompts on a card. That progression turns a diary entry into something you can say with a microphone in your hand.

Examples of cuts

Cut: a story that ends with someone vomiting. Keep: the same night’s detail about him giving away his jacket. Cut: a nickname that needs a three-minute origin. Keep: the behaviour the nickname pointed at, described in plain English. Ruthless cutting is how funny stays kind.

Delivery tips for funny lines

Pause after the punchline. Do not explain why it was funny. Do not look at the person the joke was about with a raised eyebrow that turns the room against them. Smile, move on, and let the laugh land. If a line dies, continue — digging makes it worse.

Record yourself once. If you sound like you are performing a stand-up set rather than talking to friends, soften the energy. Wedding humour should feel like a good dinner story, not a club set.

Building a shortlist of three

Write five candidates. Cut two that need the most explanation. Safety-test the remaining three with someone who knows the couple. Keep the strongest two for the draft and hold the third as a spare if a later section feels thin. Three is enough; ten is a filing cabinet, not a speech.

Linking humour to the toast

Every funny beat should still point toward why you are glad they are marrying. The last laugh before the toast should leave the room warm, not competitive. If your funniest story cannot bridge to affection, save it for the stag and pick a softer closer for the wedding.

Frequently asked questions

How many funny stories should a best man speech have?

One or two told properly beat five half-told ones.

Can I use a stag do story?

Almost never. Most of the room were not there, and the details rarely survive the safety test.

What if my funniest story is also the meanest?

Cut it. Warm beats viral.

Should every story be a joke?

No. A sincere story with one smile often lands harder than a string of punchlines.

How do I know it lands?

Tell it to one honest friend who knows the couple. Ask where they winced.
