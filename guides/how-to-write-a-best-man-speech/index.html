WRITING THE SPEECH

How to write a best man speech, step by step

THE SHORT ANSWER

Start by talking to the couple about the running order and what's off limits, gather more stories than you need, pick the two or three that show who the groom really is, then build them into a simple shape: open, introduce yourself, tell the stories, turn to the couple, toast. Aim for five to seven minutes at a UK wedding (about six is the sweet spot), write it out in full, cut hard, and rehearse out loud until you only need cue cards.

Before you write a word

Most bad speeches go wrong before the writing starts. The best man sits down with a blank page, panics, searches for jokes, and ends up with something that could be about anyone. Ten minutes of groundwork stops that.

Ask the couple four questions

  • When am I speaking, and for how long? At a traditional UK wedding the order is father of the bride, then the groom, then the best man, who closes the speeches (Debrett's). Plenty of couples change that, some now hold speeches before the meal or between courses (Hitched), and US receptions vary even more, so check.
  • Who else is speaking? If the groom is thanking everyone, you don't need to. If there's a maid of honour speech, you'll want to avoid telling the same story.
  • Is there anything I shouldn't mention? Exes, a recent bereavement, a family fall-out, a job loss. You won't always know, so ask directly.
  • How big is the room, and is there a microphone? Forty people in a pub function room and two hundred in a marquee need different delivery.

Know what the speech is for

A best man speech has three jobs: make the room laugh a few times, say something true about the groom, and leave the couple feeling celebrated. That's it. It isn't a roast, a stand-up set or a eulogy. If a line doesn't help with one of those three jobs, it goes.

Step 1: Gather far more material than you need

Give yourself a week of noticing before you write. Keep a note on your phone and add to it whenever something comes to mind. You're collecting raw material, not writing yet.

Prompts that tend to shake loose good stories:

  • How and when did you meet him? What was your first impression, and how wrong was it?
  • What's he famous for among his mates? The thing everyone says when his name comes up.
  • When did he last surprise you, in a good way?
  • What's a habit of his that drives people mad but that everyone secretly finds endearing?
  • When did you first realise this relationship was serious? What changed in him?
  • What's something he's done for someone else that he'd never mention himself?

Then ask other people. His siblings, his parents, an old schoolfriend or a colleague will have stories you've never heard, and borrowing one ("his mum tells me...") also shows you put the work in. Ask his partner too: what's the thing about him they'd want the room to know?

Step 2: Choose a theme, not a list

A list of anecdotes feels like a list. A speech with one idea running through it feels written. Look at your notes and ask what most of them have in common. Maybe he's relentlessly organised, or always late, or the person who'll drive two hours to help you move house. Pick that thread.

The theme doesn't need announcing. It just means your stories point the same way, so when you turn to the couple at the end ("and then he met someone who...") the speech lands somewhere.

Step 3: Pick two or three stories and cut the rest

This is the hard part. Most speeches have too many stories, each told too thinly. Two well-told stories beat six one-liners.

A story earns its place if:

  • At least half the room will understand it without a back-story.
  • It shows something about his character, not just something that happened.
  • His gran could hear it and the couple's new in-laws could hear it.
  • You can tell it in under a minute.

Stag do stories almost never pass. As a professional wedding speechwriter put it to BBC Radio 4, most of the room weren't there. Our best man speech jokes guide has a quick test for borderline material.

Step 4: Use a structure that works

Almost every good best man speech follows the same broad shape. It isn't a formula, it's just the order people expect, which frees you to be yourself inside it.

Step 5: Write it out in full, the way you talk

Write the whole thing as if you were telling it to a mate in the pub. Short sentences. Your own words. If you'd never say "whilst" or "betwixt" out loud, don't write them.

A few rules that make a real difference:

  • Set up, then pay off. A story needs a beat of context, a turn, and a punchline or point. Cut everything that isn't one of those three.
  • Be specific. "He's a terrible cook" is a shrug. "He once served us chicken that was still frozen in the middle and called it 'rustic'" is a laugh.
  • Name the partner early and often. A speech that only mentions the partner in the toast feels like it was written about a stag do. Include something about their personality, not just how they look today.
  • Read every line aloud as you write it. If you trip over it at your desk, you'll trip over it at the top table.

Step 6: Cut it down

Read your draft aloud with a timer. Most first drafts run long. Hitched suggests cutting your first draft by about 20%. Speaking pace at a wedding is slower than you think because of laughter and pauses, so a script that reads in five minutes at home can easily run to six or more in the room. At around 130 words a minute, six minutes is about 780 words. Our speech length guide explains how to estimate it.

What to cut first:

  1. Any story that needs a long explanation.
  2. Lists of thank-yous the groom will already make.
  3. Dictionary definitions, "Google says marriage is..." openings and borrowed quotes.
  4. The second version of a joke you've already made.
  5. Anything you've included because you're worried it's too short.

Step 7: Get one honest reader

Show it to one person who knows the groom and will tell you the truth. Not the groom, and not a group chat. Ask them three things: which bit is strongest, which bit could hurt someone, and where they got bored. Then act on it.

Step 8: Rehearse until it sounds like you

Practise out loud, standing up, at least five or six times across the final fortnight. One wedding speechwriter's advice to Brides is to rehearse until it bores you. Once it's familiar, turn it into short prompts on cue cards and rehearse from those. You want to know it well enough to look up, not to recite it from memory.

  • Record one run on your phone and listen back. You'll hear where you rush.
  • Mark pauses after punchlines. Wait for the laugh; don't talk over it.
  • Practise the first and last 30 seconds the most. They carry the speech.

Mistakes that sink speeches

  • Inside jokes. If you need to explain who "Big Dave" is, it isn't working.
  • Exes and past relationships. Never, not even as a throwaway.
  • Too much drink first. Have one if it helps, then switch to water until you've spoken.
  • Reading off a phone. It glows, it locks, and it hides your face. Use cards.
  • Forgetting the partner. The fastest way to lose the room's goodwill.
  • Making it about you. A few lines on your friendship are fine; the speech is about him and the couple.

Frequently asked questions

How do you start writing a best man speech?

Ask the couple about the running order and anything off limits, then spend a week collecting stories in a note on your phone before you write. Choose one theme and two or three stories that show the groom's character, then write a full draft in your own words.

What is the structure of a best man speech?

Opening line, a quick introduction of who you are, any thank-yous the couple asked for, two or three stories about the groom, a section about the couple, then one sincere line and a toast.

How long should a best man speech be?

Five to seven minutes suits most UK weddings, about six being ideal, and ten is the ceiling. US toasts usually run three to five. Laughter and pauses add time, so time a full read-through out loud and allow for the room.

How far in advance should I write my best man speech?

Start collecting material six to eight weeks out and have a full draft about four weeks before the wedding. That leaves time to cut, get feedback and rehearse.

Should I memorise my best man speech?

Know it well, but don't memorise it word for word. Rehearse from cue cards with short prompts so you can look up at the room without the risk of losing your place.

What should you not say in a best man speech?

Leave out exes, stag do stories, anything sexual, anything that could embarrass the couple in front of family, and in-jokes that only a few guests will understand.

Help! My best man speech is this weekend. Any tips?

Keep it to about five minutes: open, say who you are, tell one or two clean stories about the groom, say something specific about his partner, then toast. Write it out tonight, read it aloud three times, put prompts on cards, and go easy on the drink before you speak.
