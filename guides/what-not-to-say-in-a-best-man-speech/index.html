WRITING THE SPEECH

What not to say in a best man speech

THE SHORT ANSWER

Leave out exes, anything sexual, stag do stories that would embarrass the groom in front of his new family, in-jokes only a few people understand, digs at the partner, and anything about money, weight or what the wedding cost. Use a simple test for every line: if you wouldn't say it with his grandmother and his new boss sitting at the front, cut it. Then replace what you've cut with stories that show what kind of person he is.

Why it matters

A best man speech is given to the most mixed audience most people will ever face: grandparents and children, his family and the partner's family, old friends and new colleagues, some of whom have never met him. It's also usually filmed, and clips get shared. A line that would get a big laugh in the pub can land in silence at a wedding, and it's the silence people remember.

The good news is that almost every best man speech disaster comes from the same short list of topics. Avoid them and you've removed most of the risk before you've written a word.

The topics to leave out

Exes

Never mention his previous relationships, even as a joke, even if "everyone knows". It's the fastest way to make the partner and their family uncomfortable. Instead: talk about how he's changed since meeting the partner.

Anything sexual

That includes the wedding night, innuendo and "what happens on tour" hints. Grandparents and children are in the room. Instead: if you want to show the couple are well matched, tell a story about them together that you'd be happy to tell at a family lunch.

Stag do stories

What happened on the stag do should mostly stay there. A light, harmless moment can work, but anything involving drink, strangers or the groom being humiliated doesn't belong. Instead: pick the one stag do moment that shows him at his best, such as the speech he made to the group or the way he looked after someone.

Drinking and drugs

Stories that rely on how drunk he was tend to land badly with older guests and can make him look careless in front of his new family. Instead: tell a story about what he did, not how much he'd had.

In-jokes

If a story needs "you had to be there", most of the room wasn't. In-jokes leave the majority of guests watching a few people laugh. Instead: set the story up so someone who's never met him can follow it. Our best man speech jokes guide shows how.

Digs at the partner

Tease the groom gently if you like, but never the partner. Lines like "I don't know what she sees in him" are fine; anything about the partner's looks, past, family or habits isn't. Instead: give the partner one warm, specific line about who they are.

Money and the cost of the wedding

Jokes about how much the day cost, who paid for it, or the groom's salary or debts can embarrass the people who paid. Instead: thank the hosts if the couple have asked you to, and leave the money out.

Appearance and weight

Jokes about the groom's hairline might be fine with the groom. Jokes about anyone's weight, body or looks rarely are. Instead: compliment, don't compare.

Family rifts and divorces

Never joke about divorced parents, step-families or anyone who isn't speaking to anyone else. Instead: if the family situation is complicated, ask the couple whether there's anything you should avoid.

Lines that undercut you

Some phrases don't offend anyone, but they make the speech weaker. Cut them:

  • "I'm not very good at this." Apologising in advance makes the room nervous for you.
  • "I looked up best man speeches online." It tells the room the next line is borrowed.
  • "I'll keep this short." Only say it if you mean it.
  • "I didn't think it would last." Even as a joke, it's the wrong message on their wedding day.
  • "Anyway, that's it." End on the toast, not a shrug. See how to end a best man speech.
  • Borrowed one-liners. Someone has heard them at another wedding. Lines from your own stories always land better.

The grey areas

  • Swearing. Generally avoid it. One mild word in the right place can work with some crowds, but you rarely lose anything by leaving it out.
  • Teasing the groom. Light teasing is part of the job, as long as it's affectionate and ties back to something good about him. Keep it to things he'd happily laugh about with his family there.
  • Someone who has died. Check with the couple first. If they'd like a mention, keep it brief and warm, and don't put it right before a joke.
  • Religion and politics. Leave them out unless they're central to the couple and you're sure it'll be welcome.
  • Reading out messages. A short message from someone who couldn't come can be lovely. A long list of them slows the speech down; leave those for the couple to read later.
  • Talking about yourself. A line about how you met him is fine. Long stories where you're the hero aren't.

Risky lines and better ones

Most risky lines can be rescued by keeping the trait and changing the target. A few examples:

Instead ofTry
"His last girlfriend would never have put up with this.""Since he met Jess, he's started replying to messages. Within the week."
"I won't say what happened in Prague, but...""On the stag do, he made a speech to the group that was better than this one."
"He was so drunk at my birthday that...""At my birthday he insisted on making everyone toast. At four in the morning."
"I don't know how much this wedding cost, but...""I'd like to thank everyone who made today happen."
"Jess, you could have done so much better.""Jess, you've made him happier than I've ever seen him."
"Only the lads will get this one."A story with a clear set-up that anyone can follow

Each replacement keeps the warmth of the joke but removes the part that would make someone in the room wince.

What to say instead

Once you've cut the risky material, you need something to replace it. The safest and most effective approach is to pick one trait of the groom's and show it through a story or two, then turn it into a compliment at the end.

  • Choose a trait you can say out loud with pride: loyal, generous, competitive, always late, brilliant in a crisis.
  • Find a story that shows it, with a clear set-up, a turn and a point.
  • Bring in the partner: what you noticed when you first met them, or what's changed in him since.
  • Finish sincerely, then raise the toast.

Test every line before the day

Run each story and joke through three quick checks:

  1. The grandmother test. Would you say it with his grandmother and his new boss sitting at the front?
  2. The stranger test. Would someone who's never met him understand it and laugh?
  3. The partner test. Would the partner be happy to hear it, with their parents next to them?

If a line fails any of them, cut it or rewrite it. Then read the whole speech to one trusted person who knows the families but isn't the groom. If you're unsure about something specific, ask the couple. They'd much rather you checked than be surprised on the day.

Frequently asked questions

Should you mention exes in a best man speech?

No. Even as a joke, mentioning his previous relationships makes the partner and their family uncomfortable. Talk about how he's changed since meeting the partner instead.

Can you swear in a best man speech?

It's best to avoid it. There are usually children and older guests in the room, and the speech is often filmed. You rarely lose anything by leaving swearing out.

Is it OK to roast the groom?

Light, affectionate teasing is fine and expected. Keep it to things he'd happily laugh about in front of his family, and tie it back to something good about him.

Should I mention someone who has died?

Check with the couple first. If they'd like a mention, keep it short and warm, and don't follow it straight away with a joke.

Should I tell stag do stories in the speech?

Mostly no. A light, harmless moment can work, but anything involving drink, strangers or the groom being embarrassed should stay on the stag do.

What is the grandmother test?

A quick check for every line: would you say it with the groom's grandmother and his new boss sitting at the front? If not, cut it or rewrite it.
