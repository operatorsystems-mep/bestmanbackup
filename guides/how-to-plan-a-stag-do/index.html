THE STAG DO

How to plan a stag do

THE SHORT ANSWER

Start with a short conversation with the groom about who's coming, roughly when, and what he'd actually enjoy. Then set a per-head budget, pick a date well before the wedding (Hitched recommends one to three months before), choose the format (a day, a night or a weekend), book the big things in the right order, take deposits as people confirm, and plan a simple running order for the day itself. Write it all down in one place, because you'll be asked the same questions twenty times.

Start with the groom

The best stag dos are built around the groom, not around the loudest person in the group chat. Before you look at a single venue, sit down with him for twenty minutes and agree the basics:

  • What does he want? A big weekend away, a day doing something he loves, or a quiet dinner and a few drinks are all proper stag dos.
  • What doesn't he want? Ask directly. Some grooms dread fancy dress, strip clubs or a trip abroad. Better to know now.
  • Who must be there? And is there anyone he'd rather not invite? It's his call, not yours.
  • Any limits? Money, travel, drinking, time off work, or family commitments for anyone on the list.
  • Surprise or not? Most grooms like to know the date and roughly what's happening. You can still keep the details as a surprise.

It's also worth asking whether his partner has any views, particularly on timing or anything that would cause trouble at home. A two-minute check now saves an awkward conversation later.

Build the guest list

The guest list decides almost everything else: the budget, the type of activity and how much organising you'll do. Get the groom to sign it off, then keep it in one shared place.

  • Size. A group of eight is easy to book and easy to move around. Twenty or more needs group bookings, a bigger deposit and more planning.
  • Mixed groups. School friends, university friends, work friends and family often don't know each other. Plan something at the start that mixes them up.
  • Dads and older relatives. Many stag dos invite the fathers, brothers-in-law or uncles to one part of the day, such as lunch or an afternoon activity, and keep the evening for the main group.
  • The "maybes". Ask for a yes or no by a set date. A deposit is the only real yes. StagWeb says that, on average, at least three people drop out from the original invite list, so plan for it.

Pick the date

Hitched recommends holding the stag do one to three months before the wedding, and StagWeb, one of the UK's biggest stag operators, says most groups aim for at least two months before. Avoid the weekend right before the wedding: nobody wants the groom tired, broke or nursing an injury in his wedding photos, and there's usually too much else going on.

  • Check the groom's dates first, then the people he says are essential.
  • Offer three to five options in a poll rather than asking "when's good for everyone?" Hitched suggests getting the dates from the groom, letting the group vote, and locking them in six to twelve months ahead.
  • Watch out for school holidays, bank holidays and big sporting weekends, which can push prices up and make bookings harder.
  • For a trip abroad, give people more notice so they can book time off and check their passports are in date.

Set the budget before you book

Agree a per-head budget before you start booking, and base it on what the least well-off person on the list can comfortably afford, not the best-paid. People drop out of stag dos because of money far more often than because of dates.

For a sense of scale, stag operator StagWeb reported an average spend of £195 a head on UK stag dos in 2024 (£214 abroad). StagWeb says its averages cover accommodation, activities and nightlife only, and exclude transport, food and drink, and extras such as fancy dress. Yours doesn't need to be anywhere near that. Your budget should cover travel, accommodation, activities, food and the group's share of the groom's costs. Choose the format

Once you know the group, the date and the budget, the format usually picks itself.

FormatWorks well forWatch out for
A day outMixed ages, tighter budgets, people with young childrenFitting in travel time at both ends
A night outLocal groups who all live near each otherBig groups getting split up between bars
A weekend in the UKGroups who want time together without flightsAccommodation for everyone, minimum-stay rules
A trip abroadGroups with the money and the time offCost, passports, travel insurance, people who can't come

Not every stag do needs to be built around drinking. Plenty of groups go for an activity during the day (golf, go-karting, a cooking class, a match, a hike) and a good dinner in the evening. If anyone on the list doesn't drink, plan at least one main part of the day that works just as well without it.

Book in the right order

Book the things that are hardest to change first:

  1. Accommodation and travel, for anything overnight. Check the cancellation terms and whether the venue accepts stag groups at all; some don't.
  2. The main activity. Check minimum numbers, age or ID requirements, and what happens if fewer people turn up.
  3. Dinner. Large groups usually need to book well ahead, and many restaurants ask for a deposit or a set menu.
  4. Extras, such as transport between venues, a private area in a bar or anything for the groom to wear.

Keep every booking reference, deposit amount, balance due date and cancellation deadline in one list. You'll need it when people ask "what's the plan?" for the tenth time, and when someone drops out.

Collect the money without falling out

Collecting money is the part most best men hate. A few simple rules make it painless:

  • Ask for a deposit when someone says yes. It turns a "probably" into a commitment.
  • Set one clear deadline for the balance, well before your own payments are due.
  • Use one payment method and ask everyone to include their name as the reference.
  • Don't front large sums you can't afford to lose if people pay late or drop out.
  • Share a running total so everyone can see who has paid and what the money has gone on.

Hitched advises making the groom's share a group decision, especially for an expensive trip abroad. Agree it up front rather than adding it to the bill at the end.

Plan the day itself

A loose running order stops the day drifting. You don't need a minute-by-minute schedule, just the shape of it: where you're meeting, the main activity, food, the evening and how everyone gets home.

  • Build in more time than you think you need between each part. Groups move slowly.
  • Plan one or two games or moments that make the groom the centre of attention. Our stag do games guide has twelve that work with or without drink.
  • Have a quiet moment where you say a few words about the groom. Keep it to a minute.
  • Agree a rough end point. People leave happier when the night finishes on a high.

Look after everyone

Part of the best man's job on a stag do is making sure everyone, especially the groom, gets home in one piece and still friends.

  • Don't humiliate the groom. Light teasing is fine. Anything that would embarrass him in front of his partner or his family isn't.
  • Look after the people who don't know anyone. Introduce them early and put them in a team with the groom's oldest friends.
  • Respect non-drinkers. Nobody should be pressured, and every game should work with water.
  • Keep the details safe. Have the address of where you're staying, the booking references and a couple of phone numbers somewhere other than one person's phone.
  • Abroad, remind everyone to bring their passport and to sort their own travel insurance.

The week beforeSend the final running order, meeting point and timings to everyone
The week afterSettle up any refunds or extras and share the final tally

StagWeb found the average stag group books about five months (143 days) ahead, and recommends booking earlier than that to get the best choice of dates and venues. For a small local night out, you can compress all of this into a few weeks.

After the stag do

Settle up within a week while everyone still remembers what they owe, and share the final tally so nobody wonders where the money went. Then write down the best moments while they're fresh. The stag do is one of the best sources of material for your speech, as long as you only use the stories that are safe to tell in front of his grandmother. Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should you plan a stag do?

For a weekend away, start six months or more ahead; Hitched suggests locking in dates six to twelve months before. StagWeb found the average group books about five months (143 days) ahead and recommends booking earlier. A small local night out needs far less notice.

When should the stag do be?

Hitched recommends one to three months before the wedding, and StagWeb says most groups aim for at least two months before. Avoid the weekend right before it, so the groom isn't tired or out of pocket on the day.

Who organises the stag do?

Usually the best man: StagWeb's 2024 figures show the best man planned 56% of the stag dos it booked. Get the groom's input on the guest list, the date and what he'd enjoy. It's fine to hand parts of it, such as transport or games, to other groomsmen.

Does the groom pay for his own stag do?

Traditionally not. The group usually splits his share, but Hitched advises making that a group decision, especially for an expensive trip abroad.

How much does a stag do cost?

It varies hugely. StagWeb reported an average spend of £195 a head on UK stag dos in 2024, and £214 abroad, covering accommodation, activities and nightlife but not transport or food and drink. A day out or a local night can cost far less.

Should the stag do be a surprise?

Usually not completely. Agree the date, the guest list and the rough plan with the groom, and keep the details, such as the activity or the games, as the surprise.
