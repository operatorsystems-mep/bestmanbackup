NERVES AND DELIVERY

How to handle best man speech nerves

THE SHORT ANSWER

Nerves before a best man speech are normal and usually a sign you care. The biggest fixes are preparation and pace: rehearse out loud until the speech feels familiar, use cue cards rather than a phone, keep the drink light until you've spoken, breathe out slowly before you stand, and say your first line at half the speed you think you should. The room is on your side.

Why best man nerves feel so big

A best man speech is unusual. You're speaking to a room of people who matter to someone you love, many of whom you don't know, often after a few drinks, with a camera on you. It's no surprise your heart races. The good news: wedding guests are the friendliest audience you'll ever have. They want you to do well, because they want the day to go well.

Nerves themselves don't ruin speeches. Lack of preparation does. A prepared, nervous best man still gives a good speech.

Before the day: preparation that reduces nerves

Know the speech well

Brides' expert advice is to rehearse until it bores you, and to record yourself and listen back. Debrett's goes further and suggests avoiding notes altogether if you can. Most people find short cue cards the safer middle ground, and Toastmasters recommends bullet-point notecards. Rehearse out loud, standing, five or six times over the last two weeks. You're aiming for familiarity: knowing what comes next without reciting word for word. Memorising word for word creates a new fear, which is forgetting a word. Knowing the shape means you can't really lose it.

Rehearse in the conditions you'll face

  • Practise standing up, holding your cue cards.
  • Practise with a glass in the other hand for the toast.
  • Practise in front of one or two people. It's much harder than practising alone, which is the point.
  • Record yourself on your phone and listen back for speed.

Write the first and last lines word for word

These are the two moments when nerves peak. Knowing exactly what you'll say gets you started and gets you finished. Our opening lines guide and ending guide help with both.

Use cue cards, not your phone

Phones lock, glow, buzz with messages and hide your face. Cue cards with short prompts in large type let you glance down and look straight back up. Number them, and punch a hole and tie them with a treasury tag or ring if you're worried about dropping them.

Visit the room if you can

If you can see the venue beforehand, stand where you'll speak. Knowing where the microphone is and where the couple will sit removes several small unknowns.

On the day

Morning and afternoon

  • Eat properly. Adrenaline on an empty stomach makes shaking worse.
  • Go easy on the drink. One drink can take the edge off. Three can take the speech with it. Switch to water until you've spoken; the celebrating can start afterwards.
  • Keep your cards on you. Inside jacket pocket, plus a photo of them on your phone as a backup.
  • Find five quiet minutes. Read through once, out loud if you can, in a corner or a corridor.

Just before you stand

  • Breathe out slowly. Toastmasters recommends slow breathing from the diaphragm with an out-breath longer than the in-breath. Do it three or four times.
  • Move a little. A short walk or shaking out your arms helps burn off adrenaline, another tip from Toastmasters.
  • Loosen your hands and shoulders. Shake out the tension.
  • Find a friendly face. Spot two or three people who'll smile at you, ideally in different parts of the room.
  • Check the microphone. If there is one, Hitched advises not holding it too close.

While you speak

  • Wait for quiet before your first word. Count to three. It feels long; it looks confident.
  • Slow down. Wedding speechwriter Heidi Ellert-McDermott told Brides that talking too fast is a big problem. Speak at what feels like an exaggeratedly slow pace; to the room it'll sound normal.
  • Pause after laughs. Pausing gives you a moment to breathe.
  • Look up at the end of each sentence. Even a glance at the room helps.
  • If your hands shake, rest them. Put the cards on the table or hold them with both hands. Nobody's watching your hands.

If something goes wrong

You lose your place

Pause, look at your card, find the next prompt and carry on. A pause that feels endless to you lasts two seconds to the room. If you can't find it at all, skip to the next card or straight to the couple and the toast.

A joke falls flat

Keep going. Don't explain it, don't apologise. Most of the room won't notice a joke that didn't land if you don't draw attention to it. Our jokes guide explains why warm, specific humour fails less often.

Your voice cracks on the sincere bit

That's fine. Pause, take a breath, smile, carry on. An emotional wobble at the right moment is often what people remember most fondly.

Someone heckles

Smile, acknowledge it with a quick "Thanks, Uncle Mike", and carry on. You don't need a comeback.

You've had more to drink than planned

Slow down even more, stick tightly to your cards and go to the toast sooner. A shorter speech is better than a rambling one.

Is it normal to dread it for weeks?

Yes. Lots of best men lose sleep over the speech. The best cure is starting early: a finished draft four weeks out turns a vague dread into a specific task you can practise. Our step-by-step guide to writing a best man speech has a timeline.

If your anxiety about public speaking goes well beyond normal nerves, it's worth talking to your GP or a professional, and it's completely fine to discuss options with the groom. Some best men share the speech with a co-best man, or give a short toast instead. See our joint speech and short speech guides.

A simple pre-speech checklist

  • Cue cards numbered and in your pocket
  • Backup photo of the cards on your phone
  • Running order confirmed: when you're on and who hands to you
  • Names of the couple, parents and bridesmaids double-checked
  • Glass topped up with something for the toast
  • One slow breath out, then stand

Frequently asked questions

How do I calm my nerves before a best man speech?

Rehearse out loud until the speech feels familiar, eat properly, keep the drink light until you've spoken, and take a few slow breaths out before you stand. Then say your first line slowly.

Should I drink before my best man speech?

One drink is fine if it helps, but keep it light. Switch to water until you've spoken; too much drink is a common reason speeches go wrong.

What if I forget my best man speech?

Use cue cards with short prompts so you can find your place quickly. If you blank, pause, check the card and carry on, or go straight to the couple and the toast.

Is it OK to read a best man speech from notes?

Yes. Cue cards are normal and expected. Know the speech well enough to look up often, but don't feel you have to memorise it.

I get nervous talking in front of people. How do I do a wedding speech?

Keep it short and well rehearsed. Practise out loud until it's familiar, use bullet-point cue cards, breathe out slowly before you stand, and speak more slowly than feels natural. If you lose your place, pause, find your next prompt and carry on.
