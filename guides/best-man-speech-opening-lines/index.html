HOW TO START A BEST MAN SPEECH

Best man speech opening lines, and how to start strong

THE SHORT ANSWER

A good opening line gets a smile in the first fifteen seconds, tells the room who you are, and gives you a moment to settle. Self-deprecating lines, a gentle joke about the groom's instructions to you, or a nod to the speeches before yours all work well. Avoid dictionary definitions, borrowed one-liners and anything that needs explaining.

What your first 30 seconds need to do

The opening isn't about being hilarious. It's about getting the room on your side and calming your own nerves. A good start does three things:

  1. Earns a first smile or laugh. It doesn't need to be big. A warm chuckle tells you the room is with you.
  2. Says who you are. Half the guests won't know you. "For those who don't know me, I'm Dan, and I've known Tom since we were eleven" is all you need.
  3. Sets the tone. Funny, warm, a bit of both. Guests relax when they can tell what kind of speech this will be.

Write the first few lines word for word and practise them more than anything except the ending. Once you're past them, the rest of the speech gets easier.

Brides puts it simply: you only have a few seconds to grab the room's attention, so plan them.

Opening lines by style

All of these are templates. Swap the details for your own, and only use one if it sounds like something you'd actually say.

Self-deprecating

  • "For those who don't know me, I'm [name]. Tom chose me as his best man because, and I quote, 'everyone else was busy.'"
  • "I've been told the best man should be charming, witty and handsome. So I'd like to apologise in advance to everyone for this next five minutes."
  • "I was going to start with a joke, but then I remembered the groom's dress sense and decided the room had already had one."
  • "Public speaking is apparently one of people's greatest fears. Second only, I'm told, to being Tom's flatmate."

About the groom's instructions

  • "Tom gave me three rules for this speech: keep it short, keep it clean, and don't mention Magaluf. So that's the first two sorted."
  • "The groom has asked me to keep this under five minutes. He has also asked me not to tell you why."
  • "Tom's given me a list of stories I'm not allowed to tell. It was longer than this speech."

Following the speeches before yours

At a traditional UK wedding the best man speaks last, after the father of the bride and the groom (Debrett's). Traditionally he also starts by replying on behalf of the bridesmaids if the groom has toasted them. A quick nod to them is an easy laugh.

  • "Well, after two speeches that moving, I'd like to lower the tone slightly."
  • "Thank you, [groom]. That was lovely. Unfortunately for everyone, he's handed over to me."
  • "I'm the last speech, which means I'm the only thing standing between you and the bar. I'll be quick."

Warm and sincere

  • "I've known Tom for twenty years, and I've never been prouder to stand next to him than I am today."
  • "When Tom asked me to be his best man, I said yes before he finished the sentence. I then spent three months wondering what I'd agreed to."
  • "Good evening, everyone. Before I say anything else, I just want to say how good it is to see Tom this happy."

For a brother or family best man

  • "I'm Tom's brother, which means I've had thirty years to prepare for this speech, and I've still left it to last Tuesday."
  • "Most best men have to work hard to find embarrassing stories. I had to work hard to pick only two."

There's more on family speeches in our best man speech for a brother guide.

For a joint speech

  • "We're doing this together, partly so we can share the stories, and mostly so we can share the blame."
  • "Tom couldn't decide which of us to choose, which tells you everything about his decision-making."

Matching the opening to the room

The same line can storm one room and die in another. Before you choose, think about who's actually sitting in front of you.

Small, mostly friendsWarmer in-jokes, as long as you explain them in a lineAssuming the partner's family are in on it

Openings that use a prop or a moment

A small prop can buy you an easy first laugh, as long as it's quick and you've cleared it with the couple.

  • The list. Pull out a long folded sheet: "These are the stories Tom asked me not to tell." Put it away. "So, a short speech, then."
  • The phone. "Tom asked me to read out the messages from guests who couldn't make it. There weren't any. You all came. That's how much he means to people." It flips a joke into warmth.
  • The photo. If there's a screen, one childhood photo works. One. Check the venue's tech beforehand and have a plan if it fails.

Keep props to the first minute. After that, they get in the way of the stories.

Write three openings and test them

Don't settle on the first line you think of. Write three different openings: one self-deprecating, one about the groom, one sincere. Read each aloud to one honest person and watch their face. Pick the one that made them smile without you having to explain it. Keep a second one in your pocket in case the speech before yours steals your angle, which happens more than you'd think when the groom and best man both mention the same holiday.

Openings to avoid

  • "Webster's Dictionary defines marriage as..." It's the most-heard opening in wedding history.
  • Long lists of thank-yous. The groom usually covers these. If the couple ask you to thank someone, keep it to a line or two.
  • Jokes about the partner or their family. You haven't earned that yet in the first ten seconds, and maybe not at all.
  • Anything crude. The first line sets the tone for grandparents and children too.
  • "I'm not very good at this." You'll feel it, but don't say it. Start with a planned line instead.
  • Tapping the microphone and saying "Is this on?" Check it before the speeches start.

How to deliver the opening

  • Wait for quiet. Stand, smile, and wait a few seconds before you speak. It feels long; it isn't.
  • Look at the room, not your cards. You should know the first two lines by heart.
  • Pause after the first joke. Let the laugh happen. If it doesn't come, carry on calmly; nobody minds.
  • Slow down. Nerves speed you up most at the start. Say the first sentence at half the speed you think you should.

From the opening into the speech

A good opening flows straight into who you are and how you know the groom, then into your first story. A simple bridge:

"For those who don't know me, I'm Dan. I met Tom on our first day of secondary school, when he asked to borrow a pen. I'm still waiting to get it back. Which tells you most of what you need to know about Tom..."

Frequently asked questions

How do you start a best man speech?

Stand, wait for quiet, then open with a short line that gets a smile. Follow it with who you are and how you know the groom, then move into your first story.

What is a good first line for a best man speech?

Something short and in your own voice: a self-deprecating joke, a nod to the groom's rules for the speech, or a line about following the earlier speeches. Avoid dictionary definitions and borrowed one-liners.

Should the best man introduce himself?

Yes. Many guests won't know you, so say your name and how you know the groom in the first 30 seconds.

Should a best man speech start with thank-yous?

Keep them brief. The groom normally handles the main thank-yous. Traditionally the best man thanks the groom on the bridesmaids' behalf if he toasted them, and may read messages from absent guests.
