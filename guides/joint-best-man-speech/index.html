TWO BEST MEN, ONE SPEECH

Joint best man speech: how to split it, with a full example

Updated 4 November 2026

About these guides: written by Best Man Backup, an independent site for best men only.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Two best men should give one speech of about seven to ten minutes, not two speeches back to back. Split the material by era or by theme, script three handovers, and rehearse standing up at least twice.

Why couples choose joint best men

More grooms now have two best men: a brother and a best friend, two friends from different stages of life, or two people who simply couldn't be split. It's a lovely way to honour both relationships, and it halves the pressure on each of you. It does mean the speech needs more planning.

Options for structuring a joint speech

ApproachHow it worksBest for
Split by eraOne covers childhood or school, the other adult lifeA brother and a friend, or friends from different times
Split by themeEach takes a different trait or side of the groomFriends from the same group
Back-and-forthShort alternating sections, sometimes finishing each other's linesConfident pairs who rehearse well
One leads, one addsOne gives most of the speech, the other tells one story and gives the toastWhen one of you is nervous or less comfortable speaking

Pick the approach that suits your personalities. A double act needs real rehearsal. A clean split by era is simpler and still works well.

Step by step

1. Meet early and agree the basics

Before writing anything, agree:

  • Total length. Aim for about seven to ten minutes combined, roughly 900 to 1,300 words at around 130 words a minute. Speechwriting guides suggest six to eight or seven to eight minutes, and all of them warn against doubling the length. See how long a best man speech should be.
  • Tone. If one of you is naturally funny and the other is sentimental, use it, but agree the overall feel.
  • Who opens and who toasts. Often whoever opens doesn't toast, so both of you get a big moment.
  • What's off limits. Agree it together with the couple so nobody gets surprised.

2. Pool your stories

Each of you writes a list of stories and traits. Put them side by side. You'll find overlap, which tells you what the groom is really known for, and you'll find gaps each of you can fill.

3. Divide the material

Assign each story to one person. Agree a single theme so your halves point the same way. If one of you covers his reliability with a childhood story and the other with an adult one, the speech feels joined up.

4. Write separately, then stitch together

Write your parts on your own, then put them in one document and read it through together. Cut repeats, smooth the transitions and check the total length.

5. Script the handovers

Handovers are where joint speeches wobble. Write them word for word.

Dan: "...and that was the last time Tom was allowed near a barbecue. But for what happened when he moved to London, I'll hand over to someone who had to live with him."

Raj: "Thanks, Dan. Yes, I was his flatmate for three years. I've had therapy since."

6. Rehearse together, in person

At least twice, ideally standing up, with cue cards. Agree where you'll stand, who holds the microphone, and how you pass it. Practise the toast together so you don't both start it.

Who does what: a sample split

  1. Best man A: opening line, introduces both of you, quick thank-you if requested.
  2. Best man A: story from school or childhood.
  3. Handover.
  4. Best man B: story from adult life.
  5. Best man B: how the groom changed when he met his partner.
  6. Both: a short line each about the couple.
  7. Best man A or B: the toast.

A full joint best man speech example (about 7 minutes)

The names are made up. Ben is Luke's brother. Owen is the friend from university. Hannah is Luke's partner. Read it as one speech with two voices: three handovers, one running callback ("five more minutes"), a sincere turn, and a single shared toast. About 900 words, which is roughly seven minutes at 130 words a minute before laughs. Cut a story before you add one.

Ben, Luke's brother

Evening, everyone. I'm Ben, Luke's brother, which means I got here first and I have been keeping notes ever since. This is Owen. He lived with Luke at university, so he has notes of his own, and we agreed to share them rather than compete. We also agreed a rule. One speech. One toast. And nobody tells the story about the tent. Owen, that includes you.

Owen, Luke's university friend

I had not even sat down.

Ben, Luke's brother

Luke's favourite sentence, for as long as I can remember, is "five more minutes." As a child it was five more minutes of the match, five more minutes in the pool, five more minutes before anyone was allowed to turn the light out. Our mum used to stand in the hall with his shoes already in her hand. He would appear, eventually, looking as if time had personally let him down. The morning of his first Saturday job he shouted "five more minutes" down the stairs and then realised he was already late, because he had said it three times. He still got the job. They said he was keen. I have never let him forget the wording.

The useful thing about that habit is what he did with the extra minutes. If I had a school project due and I had left it too late, which was often, Luke sat at the kitchen table and stayed until it was done. Not because he was the organised one. He was not. Because he would rather be late himself than leave me with it. He has been late for almost everything. He has not once been late for me. Which is why the years I was not in the room matter. Owen watched the version of him that had to be a friend, not a brother. Over to you.

Owen, Luke's university friend

Thanks, Ben. I met Luke in a kitchen that smelled of burnt toast and ambition. He had put a sign on the fridge that said "label your food," and then he labelled nothing, including a pot that may once have been chilli. We lived together for three years. I can confirm that five more minutes survived university. It became five more minutes before a lecture, five more minutes before anyone admitted they were hungry, and, once, five more minutes in a taxi while Luke went back upstairs for a charger he had already put in his pocket. The driver was patient. Luke tipped him with the cash that was meant to be lunch, and then he ate mine. I mention it because it is the whole person. He loses the small things. He is generous with the big ones.

There was a week in second year when I was properly stuck, an essay on one side and a family worry I did not want to explain on the other. Luke did not ask me to perform the explanation. He made tea, badly, and sat on the floor with his laptop so I was not working alone. Every so often he said "five more minutes," and what he meant was that he was not going anywhere. That is the version of him Hannah met, even if she got the better tea. I have had hers. It is better.

Ben, Luke's brother

She did get the better tea. I watched the rest of it happen, which is a brother's privilege and also slightly unfair, because I had spent twenty years trying to get him out of the door and she managed it by suggesting they leave early for a walk. He was ready. Coat on. Shoes found. I have a photograph, which I will not pass round, of his face when he realised he was early. He looked suspicious of himself.

What I want to say properly is this. Luke has always been the person who stays in the room. Growing up, that was literal. He stayed at the table, on the touchline, in the hall with the shoes. Now it is the way he listens, and the way he turns up, and the way he has made space for Hannah without making a speech about it. Hannah, you have made him calmer without making him smaller. He laughs more easily with you. He plans things, which those of us who have waited in hallways can hardly believe, and he does it because the plan has you in it. I was worried, in the way brothers are, that we would lose the version of him we knew. We did not. We got him back with his shoulders down.

Owen, you saw the day-to-day. You should take him home.

Owen, Luke's university friend

From the years in the flat, the change was obvious to everyone except Luke. He started leaving on time. He started owning more than one working charger. He talked about Hannah the way he talks about the people he has already decided to keep, as if it were the most ordinary, certain thing in the world. Hannah, thank you for looking after him, and for letting the rest of us stay close while you build a life that is yours. You do not have to earn a seat in this. He already pulled one up.

Ben, Luke's brother

So we are going to finish the way we agreed, which is together, and only once. If you are able, please stand, and raise a glass.

Owen, Luke's university friend

To Luke and Hannah.

Ben, Luke's brother

To Luke and Hannah. Five more minutes would not be enough. A lifetime might be.

Why it works

  • It is one speech. Each half exists so the other half has something to answer.
  • The callback is set up in childhood, paid off in the flat, and turned sincere in the toast.
  • Three handovers are written out, so nobody has to invent the join on the night.
  • The toast is said once, by both of them, which tells the room the speech is finished.

Staging: where to stand and how to hand over

Decide the staging before you fall in love with the jokes. A joint speech comes apart in the gaps, not in the paragraphs.

  • One mic or two. One microphone, passed on the handover line, looks like one speech. Two microphones only help if you have actually rehearsed with both, and if the room can hear the person who is not currently talking stay quiet.
  • What the silent speaker does. Stand still, look at the speaker or at the couple, and do not pull a face for the front row. Hold your card. If you need to laugh, laugh after the room does.
  • One shared cue card, marked by speaker. Put B or O in the margin, large enough to see, and mark the handover lines. Two separate scripts drift apart by the second rehearsal.
  • Rehearse standing up, twice. Once for the words, once for the pass: who steps, who takes the mic, who says "please stand". Do it in the shoes you will wear if you can. Sitting down hides how long a handover actually takes.

Opening lines for a joint speech

  • "We're doing this together, partly so we can share the stories, and mostly so we can share the blame."
  • "Tom couldn't choose between us. We've taken that as a compliment, though we suspect he just couldn't make a decision."
  • "Between us, we've known Tom for forty years. He's only been nice for about six of them."

Common joint-speech mistakes

  • Two full speeches. Two back-to-back solo speeches easily pass the ten-minute ceiling most experts give.
  • Telling the same story twice. Swap drafts early.
  • One person dominating. Not always a problem if agreed, but if it's meant to be equal, check timings.
  • Long in-jokes between you. The banter between best men only works if the room's in on it.
  • No plan for the microphone. Decide who holds it and when it moves.
  • Starting the toast together. Decide who says "please raise your glasses".

When the two best men don't know each other

A brother and an old schoolfriend may barely have met. Get together, or at least have a long call, well before the wedding. Telling each other stories about the groom is the best icebreaker, and it'll make your handovers warmer on the day. If you're in different cities, a shared document plus two video rehearsals works.

Best man and best woman, or other combinations

The same principles apply to a best man and best woman, a best man and a "best person", or any pair. Use whatever titles the couple prefer and introduce each other clearly so guests know who you are to the groom.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a joint best man speech be?

About seven to ten minutes combined, roughly 900 to 1,300 words, rather than two full speeches back to back.

How do you split a joint best man speech?

Split by era (childhood and adult life), by theme, or by having one person lead and the other add a story and the toast. Agree one theme so both halves point the same way.

Who gives the toast in a joint best man speech?

Either can, but agree it in advance. Often the person who didn't open gives the toast, so both of you get a big moment.

How do you hand over in a joint best man speech?

Script the handover word for word, usually with a line that sets up the other person's story, and agree who holds the microphone.

Should two best men give one speech or two?

One speech is usually kinder on the room. Two separate speeches can work if the couple have asked for both and each one is short, but back to back they often run long. If one of you is his brother, read how to write a best man speech for your brother. If the handover is the part you dread, see how to handle best man speech nerves. For where a joint speech sits among the other speakers, see wedding speech order.

Can a groom have more than one best man?

Yes. Traditionally there's one, but Hitched notes there's no strict rule, and many grooms choose two to honour a brother and a best friend equally.
